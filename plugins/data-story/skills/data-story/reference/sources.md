# Sources

The flow follows Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's *Storytelling with Data: A Data Visualization Guide for Business Professionals* (Wiley, 2015), one lesson per step, in the order her chapter 8, "pulling it all together", runs them on one example. The table rules in `visuals/table.md` follow Stephen Few's *Show Me the Numbers*, and the measurement rules in `measurements.md` follow Martin Kleppmann's *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*; both are mapped below. Pages for Few are the second edition. The rules here are paraphrased, and the quotations are short phrases from the book. Her chapter titles are printed in lowercase. The worked examples in `visuals/table.md`, `visuals/line.md` and `chapters/story.md`, and the two charts in `assets/`, are this skill's own and are not from the book.

## File by file

- `SKILL.md`: the order of the steps is chapter 8's six lessons (understand the context, choose an appropriate display, eliminate clutter, draw attention where you want it, think like a designer, tell a story), with the words handed to another skill and a check added. The chooser is chapter 2, "choosing an effective visual", which goes through the dozen visuals she uses and the ones to avoid (pies, donuts, 3D, a second y-axis); her answer to which graph to use is "whatever will be easiest for your audience to read". Her ordering of categories is in chapter 2 and case study 3 of chapter 9, and the alternatives to a pie are case study 5. The check is from the test of showing the piece to someone with no context (chapters 2, 4, 7 and 10), her sidebar on ethics in chapter 2, and McKee's questions in chapter 7.
- `chapters/context.md`: chapter 1, "the importance of context". The two questions to answer before anything else are "Who is your audience? What do you need them to know or do?", and only then how data helps make the point. The chapter also covers the specific audience and the decision maker, the relationship with the audience, the action words, live presentation against a written document, tone, the questions to ask the person who requested the work, exploratory against explanatory analysis (the hundred oysters and the two pearls), the 3-minute story, and the Big Idea, which Knaflic takes from Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* (2010): it "must articulate your unique point of view", "must convey what's at stake", and "must be a complete sentence". Her sidebar on nonsupporting data is the reason the numbers that cut against the point stay in. The brief is this skill's stand-in for her storyboard.
- `chapters/declutter.md`: chapter 3, "clutter is your enemy!". Clutter is "visual elements that take up space but don't increase understanding". The chapter covers cognitive load, the Gestalt principles of visual perception, alignment, white space, and a chart decluttered in six steps. She credits Edward Tufte for the data-ink ratio. The reading speed of rotated text is from Wigdor and Balakrishnan (2005), cited there.
- `chapters/focus.md`: chapter 4, "focus your audience's attention". Preattentive attributes (size, color, position on the page), pushing everything to the background first, color used sparingly and consistently, designing for colorblind readers, and the test "Where are your eyes drawn?". The hawk among pigeons is Colin Ware's, from *Information Visualization* (2004). The one-tenth limit on highlighting is from Lidwell, Holden, and Butler's *Universal Principles of Design*, cited in chapter 5.
- `chapters/design.md`: chapter 5, "think like a designer": affordances (highlight the important, eliminate distractions, a clear hierarchy), accessibility (don't overcomplicate it, and text is your friend), aesthetics, and acceptance. "If there is a conclusion you want your audience to reach, state it in words", the action title in place of a descriptive one, titles on every chart and axis, annotation on the data, and plain language. Chapter 6, "dissecting model visuals", shows these choices made on five charts; the two charts in `assets/` play that part here.
- `chapters/story.md`: chapter 7, "lessons in storytelling". Beginning, middle, and end; conflict and tension; the audience as the main character; chronological order against leading with the ending; repetition; and the checks she calls horizontal logic, vertical logic, and reverse storyboarding. What the reader wants and what is in the way are Robert McKee's questions and Cliff Atkinson's, from *Beyond Bullet Points*, as she gives them. The alternatives come from her list of what the middle of a story can hold, which includes the options and the benefits of the one recommended. The objection comes from her sidebar on nonsupporting data in chapter 1 and from the question about risks in her list for the requester. She also draws on Kurt Vonnegut. The live and circulated versions of one graph are case study 2 in chapter 9, and chapter 8 builds one graph up across a five-minute talk.
- `visuals/`: chapter 2, one file per row of the chooser, covering her dozen: simple text (`simple-text.md`), the table and the heatmap (`table.md`), the line graph (`line.md`), the slopegraph (`slopegraph.md`), vertical and horizontal bars (`bars.md`), stacked vertical and horizontal bars (`stacked-bars.md`), the waterfall (`waterfall.md`), the scatterplot (`scatterplot.md`), and the square area graph (`square-area.md`). The zero baseline for bars is chapter 2's, where her example is a tax rate drawn on an axis that starts above zero; the numbers in `bars.md` are this skill's. The tangle of lines in `line.md` is case study 4 of chapter 9, the spaghetti graph.
- `reference/chart-defaults.md`: the greys, the accent, and the sizes follow her stated habits (grey with one blue, and orange for the negative end), but the exact values are this skill's. The dark background is case study 1 of chapter 9.

## Where this skill goes beyond the book

- The book is written for people making slides and reports with a graphing application. The guidance for markdown and plain text, the `<details>` block as the appendix, and painting a white background for dark-themed pages are this skill's translation.
- Using a subagent with no context as the fresh pair of eyes is this skill's way of doing what the book asks a friend or colleague to do.
- Reporting the brief with the result is this skill's version of getting the requester to agree to the storyboard.
- The three sentences are this skill's compression of her three acts and her 3-minute story. Writing the figures under each heading, and planning a live step as screen, change, and sentence, are this skill's ways of doing her checks before the piece is built.
- The three limits on a claim in step 8 are this skill's: what totals say about individuals, what one measurement says about a lasting effect, and what coincidence says about cause. The book asks that the telling be true and leaves the tests to the reader.
- The order of the chooser's rows, taking the higher row when two fit, and the Visual line that says why not the row above are this skill's. So is reading a visual's file only once the chooser has picked it.
- The construction rules in `slopegraph.md`, `stacked-bars.md`, `waterfall.md`, `scatterplot.md`, and `square-area.md` (labels at both ends, the floating steps, the reference line, sides drawn to the square root) are this skill's readings of her examples, not rules she states.
- Handing the words to `write-with-style`, with a list of what must survive the revision, is this skill's.

## Stephen Few, *Show Me the Numbers* (Analytics Press, 2nd edition, 2012)

- Table or graph (the chooser): chapter 3, "Differing Roles of Tables and Graphs" (pages 45 and 51). A table when "The display will be used to look up individual values", to compare individual values "but not entire series of values", when "Precise values are required", when the data "involves more than one unit of measure", or when summary and detail values appear together. A graph when "The message is contained in the shape of the values" (patterns, trends, exceptions) or it shows relationships among whole sets of values.
- A table is read (`visuals/table.md`, opening): chapter 3 (page 44): a table's values are "encoded as text, which we can understand directly, without translation", where a graph's are visually encoded. His paper "Use-Based Types of Quantitative Display" (2012) says tables "communicate primarily through verbal channels (words and numbers that must be read)".
- Compared values side by side: chapter 8, "Table Design" (page 184): "Place columns containing data that should be compared close together."
- The difference computed: chapter 8 (page 168): derived values (a difference, a percentage) are a column of their own, placed "just to the right of the column from which they were derived". His paper "Displaying Change Between Two Points in Time" (2014) makes the graph-side case: showing the change itself lets a reader "compare amounts of change more easily and accurately", at the cost of losing sight of the actual values, which is why this skill keeps both the values and the Δ.
- Right-aligned numbers, left-aligned labels: chapter 8 (pages 171 to 173): quantitative values "should always be aligned to the right", with "both the decimal point and the final digit" lined up; text is "aligned to the left because we read from left to right"; a header is aligned the way its column is.
- One precision per column: chapter 8 (pages 171 and 175): express each value "using the same number of decimal digits", and "The level of precision should not exceed the level needed".
- Units in the header: chapter 8 (pages 174 and 175): a unit, a scale or a rounding is declared "in the title or header" ("U.S. dollars in thousands"), and his examples carry a currency sign on summary rows only. Keeping every unit out of the cells is this skill's reading of that practice, not a sentence of his.
- Repeated labels blank: chapter 8 (page 182): it "isn't necessary to repeat the name of the state on each row"; the blanks give "an obvious visual cue" that a new group starts.
- White space over rules: chapter 8 (pages 162, 163 and 183): "Use white space alone whenever space allows", "Avoid grids altogether", and whatever support remains is subdued "in relation to the data". The data-ink idea is Edward Tufte's (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 1983); Few credits him for it in "Elegance Through Simplicity" (2004).
- Meaningful order: chapter 8 (pages 170 and 184): "Whenever categorical subdivisions have a meaningful order, sort them in that order"; alphabetical order is for looking up items that have no natural order.
- Summary rows last: chapter 8 (pages 178 to 181): a column summary "appears in a separate row beneath the last row of detailed values", visually distinct from the detail.

## Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* (O'Reilly, 2017; 2nd edition with Chris Riccomini, 2026)

The material is the section "Describing Performance": in the first edition under Scalability in chapter 1, "Reliable, Scalable, and Maintainable Applications"; in the second edition a section of chapter 2, "Defining Nonfunctional Requirements", with the subsections "Latency and Response Time", "Average, Median, and Percentiles" and "Use of Response Time Metrics".

- A measurement is a distribution (`measurements.md`, opening): think of response time "not as a single number, but as a distribution of values".
- Median over mean: the mean "doesn't tell you how many users actually experienced that delay", so "Usually it is better to use percentiles"; "The median is also known as the 50th percentile", and p95, p99 and p999 are "also known as tail latencies".
- Why the tail matters: the slowest requests often belong to "the most valuable customers", while past some percentile "the benefits are diminishing". The second edition adds that the mean still serves to estimate throughput limits.
- Percentiles do not average: averaging percentiles "is mathematically meaningless"; the way to aggregate is "to add the histograms" (the "Percentiles in Practice" sidebar in the first edition, "Computing Percentiles" in the second).
- Measure at the client: head-of-line blocking means "it is important to measure response times on the client side", and, in the first edition, a load generator should "keep sending requests independently of the response time".
- Throughput and response time are different measures: for a batch system "we usually care about throughput", for an online system "what's usually more important is the service's response time". That they are reported each on its own, never one implied from the other, is this skill's rule, not a sentence of his.
- Latency against response time: "Latency is the duration that a request is waiting to be handled", while response time adds "network delays and queueing delays" to the service time (the first edition's sidebar; the second edition words latency as time a request is not being actively processed).
- Cost: the second edition ties throughput to the resources needed "and hence the cost of serving a particular workload". Putting cost in a column next to the time it buys, with its own change, is this skill's rule.
- The book gives no rule about how many runs to make or how to report their spread; the rules under "The runs" come from the worked example's method, not from the book.

## The worked example's method

These rules are this skill's own, first worked out by rebuilding a benchmark table and now shown in the worked example in `visuals/table.md`, not taken from the books: the takeaway sentence carries what limits the result; one row per configuration, grouped and then ordered by the swept condition; the change in time next to the change in cost, each with its own Δ; the statistic, the run count, the spread and the machine in a line below the table; interleaved runs; an overlap statement as the spread; a difference inside the spread reported as no measurable difference; slow runs reported rather than dropped.

## The evals

The eval cases in `evals/` that test this skill on the book's own examples use each example's situation and numbers, with the prompts, notes, and rubrics written for this repo: the pilot summer science program (chapters 1 and 9), the ticket backlog and the hiring request (chapters 3 to 5), the top ten design concerns (chapter 4), retail prices for five products (chapter 8), the bar chart of the top tax rate (chapter 2), and, from chapter 2's tour of visuals, employee feedback across two survey years (the slopegraph), a team's headcount over a year (the waterfall), and cost per mile against miles driven (the scatterplot). Where a figure in the book gives no values, they were read off the chart.
