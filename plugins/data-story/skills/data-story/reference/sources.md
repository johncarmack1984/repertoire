# Sources

Every step in this skill follows a chapter of Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's *Storytelling with Data: A Data Visualization Guide for Business Professionals* (Wiley, 2015). The rules here are paraphrased, and the quotations are short phrases from the book. Her chapter titles are printed in lowercase. The worked examples in `SKILL.md` and the two charts in `assets/` are this skill's own and are not from the book.

## Step by step

- Step 1, context: chapter 1, "the importance of context". The two questions to answer before anything else are "Who is your audience? What do you need them to know or do?", and only then how data helps make the point. The chapter also covers the specific audience and the decision maker, the relationship with the audience, the action words, live presentation against a written document, tone, and the list of questions to ask the person who requested the work.
- Step 2, the point: chapter 1. Exploratory against explanatory analysis (the hundred oysters and the two pearls), the 3-minute story, and the Big Idea, which Knaflic takes from Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* (2010): it "must articulate your unique point of view", "must convey what's at stake", and "must be a complete sentence". Her sidebar on nonsupporting data is the reason the numbers that cut against the point stay in. The brief is this skill's stand-in for her storyboard.
- Step 3, form: chapter 2, "choosing an effective visual", which goes through the dozen visuals she uses and the ones to avoid (pies, donuts, 3D, a second y-axis). Her answer to which graph to use is "whatever will be easiest for your audience to read". The zero baseline for bars, with the 35 against 39.6 example, and the ordering of categories are in the same chapter. Case studies 3, 4, and 5 in chapter 9 cover logical order, the tangle of lines she calls a spaghetti graph, and alternatives to pies.
- Step 4, clutter: chapter 3, "clutter is your enemy!". Clutter is "visual elements that take up space but don't increase understanding". The chapter covers cognitive load, the Gestalt principles of visual perception, alignment, white space, and a chart decluttered in six steps. She credits Edward Tufte for the data-ink ratio. The reading speed of rotated text is from Wigdor and Balakrishnan (2005), cited there.
- Step 5, focus: chapter 4, "focus your audience's attention". Preattentive attributes (size, color, position on the page), pushing everything to the background first, color used sparingly and consistently, designing for colorblind readers, and the test "Where are your eyes drawn?". The hawk among pigeons is Colin Ware's, from *Information Visualization* (2004). The one-tenth limit on highlighting is from Lidwell, Holden, and Butler's *Universal Principles of Design*, cited in chapter 5.
- Step 6, words: chapter 5, "think like a designer". "If there is a conclusion you want your audience to reach, state it in words", the action title in place of a descriptive one, titles on every chart and axis, annotation on the data, and plain language. Chapter 6, "dissecting model visuals", shows these choices made on five charts.
- Step 7, story: chapter 7, "lessons in storytelling". Beginning, middle, and end; conflict and tension; the audience as the main character; chronological order against leading with the ending; repetition; and the checks she calls horizontal logic, vertical logic, and reverse storyboarding. She draws on Robert McKee, Kurt Vonnegut, and Cliff Atkinson's *Beyond Bullet Points*. The live and circulated versions of one graph are case study 2 in chapter 9.
- Step 8, the check: the test of showing the piece to someone with no context is in chapters 2, 4, 7, and 10. The question of whether the telling is true to the data comes from her sidebar on ethics in chapter 2 and from McKee's questions in chapter 7.
- The worked order of all six lessons on one example is chapter 8, "pulling it all together".

## Where this skill goes beyond the book

- The book is written for people making slides and reports with a graphing application. The guidance for markdown and plain text, the `<details>` block as the appendix, and painting a white background for dark-themed pages are this skill's translation.
- Using a subagent with no context as the fresh pair of eyes is this skill's way of doing what the book asks a friend or colleague to do.
- Reporting the brief with the result is this skill's version of getting the requester to agree to the storyboard.
- The three limits on a claim in step 8 are this skill's: what totals say about individuals, what one measurement says about a lasting effect, and what coincidence says about cause. The book asks that the telling be true and leaves the tests to the reader.
- The chart defaults in `design.md` (the greys, the accent, the sizes) follow her stated habits, grey with one blue and orange for the negative end, but the exact values are this skill's.

## The evals

The eval cases in `evals/` that test this skill on the book's own examples use each example's situation and numbers, with the prompts, notes, and rubrics written for this repo: the pilot summer science program (chapters 1 and 9), the ticket backlog and the hiring request (chapters 3 to 5), the top ten design concerns (chapter 4), retail prices for five products (chapter 8), and the bar chart of the top tax rate (chapter 2). Where a figure in the book gives no values, they were read off the chart.
