# Usage

Rules 1 to 7 and Chapter IV of the 1918 text: the punctuation of ordinary sentences and a few matters of form, read for prose in markdown. Rules 3 to 6 "cover the most important principles in the punctuation of ordinary sentences". Rule 8 (dividing words at line-ends) and the manuscript instructions in Chapter IV are left out; `sources.md` says why.

## 1. Form the possessive singular of nouns with 's

"Follow this rule whatever the final consonant": *Charles's friend*, *Burns's poems*, *the witch's malice*. The exceptions are ancient proper names in *-es* and *-is*, *Jesus'*, and forms such as *for conscience' sake*; *Achilles' heel* and *Moses' laws* are commonly replaced by *the heel of Achilles* and *the laws of Moses*. The pronominal possessives *hers*, *its*, *theirs*, *yours*, and *oneself* "have no apostrophe".

## 2. In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last

*red, white, and blue*; *honest, energetic, but headstrong*; "He opened the letter, read it, and made a note of its contents." The names of business firms drop the last comma: *Brown, Shipley and Company*. *Etc.* always takes a comma before it, "even if only a single term comes before it".

## 3. Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas

"The best way to see a country, unless you are pressed for time, is to travel on foot." When the interruption is slight, both commas may go, but the writer "must never omit one comma and leave the other": "Marjorie's husband, Colonel Nelson paid us a visit yesterday." and "My brother you will be pleased to hear, is now in perfect health." are "indefensible".

- A non-restrictive clause, one that adds a statement that could stand as a sentence of its own, takes commas: "The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more and more interested." So do such clauses with *when* and *where*: "Nether Stowey, where Coleridge wrote The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, is a few miles from Bridgewater."
- A restrictive clause, one that limits the word it follows, takes none: "The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place."
- An introductory or trailing phrase or dependent clause is set off by commas on the same principle.
- When a conjunction comes before a parenthetic expression, the first comma goes before the conjunction: "He saw us coming, and unaware that we had learned of his treachery, greeted us with a smile."
- *Etc.* is followed by a comma, except at the end of a sentence.

## 4. Place a comma before and or but introducing an independent clause

"The early records of the city have disappeared, and the story of its first years can no longer be reconstructed." "The situation is perilous, but there is still one chance of escape." The same comma goes before *as* (meaning *because*), *for*, *or*, *nor*, and *while* (meaning *and at the same time*) when they join two clauses. When a dependent clause or an introductory phrase that needs its own comma opens the second clause, the conjunction takes no comma after it: "The situation is perilous, but if we are prepared to act promptly, there is still one chance of escape."

*And* "is the least specific of connectives": it says that a relation exists without saying which. When the relation is cause or concession, subordinate: "As the early records of the city have disappeared, the story of its first years can no longer be reconstructed."; "Although the situation is perilous, there is still one chance of escape." An occasional loose sentence is welcome; a series of them is Rule 14's fault.

## 5. Do not join independent clauses by a comma

Two grammatically complete clauses with no conjunction between them take a semicolon or a period: "Stevenson's romances are entertaining; they are full of exciting adventures." With a conjunction, a comma (Rule 4): "Stevenson's romances are entertaining, for they are full of exciting adventures." An adverb such as *accordingly*, *besides*, *then*, *therefore*, or *thus* is not a conjunction, so the semicolon stays. Very short clauses alike in form may take a comma: "Man proposes, God disposes."

Strunk counted *so* among those adverbs and wanted a semicolon before it. Today *so* joins two clauses as a conjunction, with a comma. His advice against overusing it still applies, and his fix still reads well: "As I had never been in the place before, I had difficulty in finding my way about."

## 6. Do not break sentences in two

"In other words, do not use periods for commas." In "I met them on a Cunard liner several years ago. Coming home from Liverpool to New York." the first period "should be replaced by a comma, and the following word begun with a small letter." An emphatic word or phrase may stand as a sentence ("Again and again he called out. No reply."), provided the emphasis is warranted and the writer "will not be suspected of a mere blunder in punctuation."

## 7. A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject

"Walking slowly down the road, he saw a woman accompanied by two children." The phrase belongs to *he*; to give it to the woman, recast: "He saw a woman, accompanied by two children, walking slowly down the road." The rule covers any phrase that opens a sentence: a participle after a conjunction or a preposition, a noun in apposition, an adjective, or an adjective phrase.

- "On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him at the station." → "When he arrived (or, On his arrival) in Chicago, his friends met him at the station."
- "A soldier of proved valor, they entrusted him with the defence of the city." → "A soldier of proved valor, he was entrusted with the defence of the city."
- "Young and inexperienced, the task seemed easy to me." → "Young and inexperienced, I thought the task easy."
- "Without a friend to counsel him, the temptation proved irresistible." → "Without a friend to counsel him, he found the temptation irresistible."

Sentences that break this rule "are often ludicrous": "Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap."

## Chapter IV: A few matters of form

- **Numerals.** Dates and other serial numbers go in figures or Roman numerals, never spelled out: *August 9, 1918*; *Chapter XII*; *Rule 3*.
- **Parentheses.** Punctuate the sentence outside the parentheses as if they were absent, and the expression inside as if it stood alone, dropping its final stop unless that is a question mark or an exclamation point: "I went to his house yesterday (my third attempt to see him), but he had left town."; "He declares (and why should we doubt his good faith?) that he is now certain of success." A wholly detached sentence in parentheses keeps its stop inside them.
- **Quotations.** A formal quotation cited as evidence follows a colon, in quotation marks: *The provision of the Constitution is: "No tax or duty shall be laid on articles exported from any state."* One in apposition, or the object of a verb, follows a comma: *Aristotle says, "Art is an imitation of nature."* A line of verse or more starts a fresh line without quotation marks; in markdown, a blockquote. A quotation introduced by *that* is indirect and takes no quotation marks: "Keats declares that beauty is truth, truth beauty." Proverbs, familiar phrases of literary origin, colloquialisms, and slang take none either.
- **References.** "As a general practice, give the references in parenthesis or in footnotes, not in the body of the sentence", and abbreviate titles that recur. In markdown, a link or a parenthesis does this; the sentence stays about its subject, not about where to find it.
- **Titles.** Italics with capitalized initials: *The Iliad*, *As You Like It*, *A Tale of Two Cities*; drop the opening *A* or *The* after a possessive (*Dickens's Tale of Two Cities*). In markdown, `*Title*`.
