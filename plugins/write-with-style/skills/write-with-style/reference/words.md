# Words

Rule 13's wordy forms and Chapter V, "Words and Expressions Commonly Misused". Chapter V opens with its own principle: many of its entries "are not so much bad English as bad style, the commonplaces of careless writing", and "the proper correction is likely to be not the replacement of one word or set of words by another, but the replacement of vague generality by definite statement." Put to work as a test: a word that could sit in any sentence adds nothing to this one, so say the definite thing it stands for. Entries marked *Changed since 1918* follow today's usage, and what they allow is no fault today.

## Rule 13: wordy forms

- the question as to whether → whether (the question whether)
- there is no doubt but that → no doubt (doubtless)
- used for fuel purposes → used for fuel
- he is a man who → he
- in a hasty manner → hastily
- this is a subject which → this subject
- His story is a strange one. → His story is strange.
- *The fact that* "should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs":
  - owing to the fact that → since (because)
  - in spite of the fact that → though (although)
  - call your attention to the fact that → remind you (notify you)
  - I was unaware of the fact that → I was unaware that (did not know)
  - the fact that he had not succeeded → his failure
  - the fact that I had arrived → my arrival
- *Who is*, *which was* and the like "are often superfluous": "His brother, who is a member of the same firm" → "His brother, a member of the same firm"; "Trafalgar, which was Nelson's last battle" → "Trafalgar, Nelson's last battle".

## Chapter V

- **All right.** Two words. Idiomatic as a detached phrase meaning "Agreed" or "Go ahead"; "In other uses better avoided."
- **As good or better than.** Rearrange: "My opinion is as good or better than his." → "My opinion is as good as his, or better (if not better)."
- **As to whether.** *Whether* is enough.
- **Bid.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk's past tense *bade* is archaic; *bid* serves.
- **Case.** Usually unnecessary: "In many cases, the rooms were poorly ventilated." → "Many of the rooms were poorly ventilated."; "It has rarely been the case that any mistake has been made." → "Few mistakes have been made."
- **Certainly.** Used "to intensify any and every statement", a mannerism "bad in speech, is even worse in writing". This skill reads it as the rule for every intensifier spent on every statement.
- **Character**, **nature.** Often redundant: "Acts of a hostile character" → "Hostile acts". *Nature* is also vague: in "poems about nature" the reader "cannot tell whether the poems have to do with natural scenery, rural life, the sunset, the untracked wilderness, or the habits of squirrels."
- **Claim.** With a noun object it means *lay claim to*; "Not to be used as a substitute for declare, maintain, or charge."
- **Clever.** Overused; "best restricted to ingenuity displayed in small matters."
- **Compare.** *Compare to* points out resemblance between things of a different order (life compared to a pilgrimage); *compare with* points out differences between things of the same order (Congress compared with the British Parliament).
- **Consider.** No *as* when it means "believe to be": "I consider him thoroughly competent."
- **Dependable.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk called it a needless substitute for *reliable*; it is standard today.
- **Due to.** Belongs to a noun ("losses due to preventable fires"), not to a clause as an adverb, where *because of*, *through*, or *owing to* serves: "He lost the first game, due to carelessness." takes *because of*.
- **Effect.** As a noun, *result*; as a verb, *to bring about*. Not *affect*, which means "to influence". The vague noun ("a charming effect was produced by") gives way to the definite meaning.
- **Etc.** Not of persons; not when the reader "would be left in doubt as to any important particulars"; never at the end of a list that opens with *such as* or *for example*.
- **Fact.** Only for "matters of a kind capable of direct verification, not of matters of judgment": that lead melts at a certain temperature is a fact; that a climate is delightful is not, "however incontestable".
- **Factor**, **feature.** Hackneyed; say the thing itself. "His superior training was the great factor in his winning the match." → "He won the match by being better trained."; for "A feature of the entertainment especially worthy of mention was the singing of Miss A.", tell what Miss A. sang, or how she sang it.
- **Fix.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk kept it to *fasten* or *make firm*; *fix* meaning *mend* or *arrange* is standard today.
- **He is a man who.** "He is a man who is very ambitious." → "He is very ambitious."; "Spain is a country which I have always wanted to visit." → "I have always wanted to visit Spain."
- **However.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk kept *however* meaning *nevertheless* out of first place in its sentence or clause. An opening *however* is standard today and gets no mark, though his placement still reads well: "At last, however, we succeeded in reaching camp."
- **Kind of**, **sort of.** Only in the literal sense ("Amber is a kind of fossil resin"), not for *rather* or *something like*.
- **Less.** Quantity; *fewer* is number: "He had less men than in the previous campaign." → "He had fewer men than in the previous campaign." A round number read as an amount may take *less*: "less than a hundred".
- **Line**, **along these lines.** Overworked: "Mr. B. also spoke along the same lines." → "Mr. B. also spoke, to the same effect."; "He is studying along the line of French literature." → "He is studying French literature."
- **Literal**, **literally.** Not in support of exaggeration: "A literal flood of abuse" → "A flood of abuse"; "Literally dead with fatigue" → "Almost dead with fatigue (dead tired)".
- **Lose out.** *Changed since 1918.* Standard today.
- **Most.** Not for *almost*: "Most everybody" → "Almost everybody".
- **Near by.** *Changed since 1918.* Spelled *nearby* today, and standard as an adverb and as an adjective.
- **Oftentimes**, **ofttimes.** *Often*.
- **One hundred and one.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk kept the *and*; American usage often drops it. Follow the house style.
- **One of the most.** Not to open an essay or a paragraph: "simply threadbare and forcible-feeble."
- **People.** *Changed since 1918.* Strunk objected to *people* with a number ("six people"); it is standard today.
- **Phase.** A stage of transition or development ("the phases of the moon"), not an aspect or a topic.
- **Possess.** Not for *have* or *own*: "He possessed great courage." → "He had great courage (was very brave)."
- **Respective**, **respectively.** "May usually be omitted with advantage": "Works of fiction are listed under the names of their respective authors." → "Works of fiction are listed under the names of their authors."
- **So.** Not as an intensifier ("so good", "so warm"). For *so* joining two clauses, see `usage.md`, Rule 5.
- **State.** "Restrict it to the sense of express fully or clearly", never as a stand-in for *say* or *remark*.
- **Student body.** *Students*: "A member of the student body" → "A student". Any phrase that means no more than a simple word gives way to the word.
- **System.** Often needless: "Dayton has adopted the commission system of government." → "Dayton has adopted government by commission."; "The dormitory system" → "Dormitories".
- **Thanking you in advance.** Reads as if the writer meant "It will not be worth my while to write to you again." Thank the reader, and thank them again once the favor is done.
- **They.** *Changed since 1918.* For a person whose gender is unknown or unspecified, and after *each*, *everyone*, *anybody*, or *someone*, write *they*. Strunk's "Use he with all the above words" is 1918 usage.
- **Very.** "Use this word sparingly. Where emphasis is necessary, use words strong in themselves."
- **Viewpoint.** *Changed since 1918.* Standard today. Strunk's warning against using *point of view* for a mere *view* or *opinion* still applies.
- **While.** Best kept to its strict sense, "during the time that", not a loose stand-in for *and*, *but*, or *although*: "The office and salesrooms are on the ground floor, while the rest of the building is devoted to manufacturing." → "The office and salesrooms are on the ground floor; the rest of the building is devoted to manufacturing." As *although*, only where it "leads to no ambiguity or absurdity".
- **Whom.** *Who* when it is the subject of the following verb: "His brother, whom he said would send him the money" → "His brother, who he said would send him the money".
- **Worth while.** "Overworked as a term of vague approval"; strictly for actions: "His books are not worth while." → "His books are not worth reading." *Changed since 1918:* the spelling is *worthwhile* today.
- **Would.** Habitual action needs no *would*: "Once a year he would visit the old mansion." → "Once a year he visited the old mansion." *Changed since 1918:* Strunk required *should* in a first-person conditional, and *would* is standard there today: *I would not have succeeded without his help*.

## Chapter VI, kept for meaning

Chapter VI lists words often misspelled; a spelling checker catches those today. It passes the ones that are spelled right but meant wrong, so keep these apart: *affect* and *effect*, *lose* and *loose*, *led* and *lead*, *too* and *to*, *latter* and *later*, *principal* and *principle*, *advice* and *advise*, *preceding* and *proceeding*. *Changed since 1918:* *to-day*, *to-night*, and *to-morrow* are *today*, *tonight*, and *tomorrow*, and *any one*, *every one*, and *some one* are *anyone*, *everyone*, and *someone*, except where *one* counts (*every one of them*).
