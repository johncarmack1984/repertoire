# Sources

William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (privately printed, Ithaca, New York, 1918; Press of W. P. Humphrey, Geneva, New York). The 1918 text is in the public domain, so the reference files quote it freely. Strunk meant his rule numbers to be cited ("The numbers of the sections may be used as references in correcting manuscript"), and this skill cites them that way rather than by page.

## Other editions

The 1920 edition (Harcourt, Brace, New York; copyright 1918 and 1919), the text on Project Gutenberg as ebook #37134, renumbers the rules. It drops Rule 8 on line-end division, so 1918's Rules 9 to 12 become 8 to 11, and it adds a new Rule 12, "Use definite, specific, concrete language"; Rules 13 to 18 keep their numbers. It also adds a chapter of exercises. In the 1918 text, the point of that new rule rests in Rule 12's "Make definite assertions" and in Chapter V's call for "the replacement of vague generality by definite statement", which is where this skill keeps it. The editions revised by E. B. White from 1959 on renumber again and remain under copyright; this skill draws on none of their text.

The quotations correct three slips in the typeset copy of the 1918 text this skill was built from: "statments" in Rule 3, "panish" in Rule 15, and the comma missing from Rule 2's own example, "He opened the letter, read it, and made a note of its contents."

## Departures from 1918 usage

- **They** (Chapter V). Strunk: "Use he with all the above words, unless the antecedent is or must be feminine." *The Chicago Manual of Style* (18th edition, 2024) accepts singular *they* for a person whose gender is unknown or unspecified, and the AP Stylebook has allowed it in limited cases since 2017. This skill writes *they*.
- **Would** (Chapter V). Strunk required *should* in a first-person conditional ("I should not have succeeded without his help") and in indirect quotation. In American usage, *would* is standard in both. The entry's other half, that habitual action needs no *would*, still applies.
- **So** joining clauses (Rule 5). Strunk treated *so* as an adverb needing a semicolon; today it works as a conjunction and takes a comma, as *and* and *but* do under Rule 4. His advice against overusing it stands.
- **The comma before *Jr.*** (Rule 3). Strunk set it off with commas; current style guides no longer require them. Rule 3 in `usage.md` keeps only what he says about *etc.*
- **Spellings** (Chapter VI). *To-day*, *to-night*, and *to-morrow* are now *today*, *tonight*, and *tomorrow*; *any one*, *every one*, and *some one* are *anyone*, *everyone*, and *someone*, except where *one* counts.
- **Words now standard** (Chapter V): *dependable*, *viewpoint*, *nearby* (Strunk's *near by*), *lose out*, *fix* in the sense of mend or arrange, *people* with a number, *however* at the start of a sentence, and *worthwhile* as one word. *Bid*, with its past *bade*, is archaic. The *and* in *one hundred and one* is a matter of house style.
- **Rule 8**, dividing words at line-ends. Markdown, HTML, and every editor reflow text, so writers no longer divide words by hand.
- **Chapter IV's manuscript instructions**: a blank line after the title, and beginning on the first line of ruled paper. Markdown headings take care of the first; the second is for paper.
- **Chapter VI's spelling list.** A spelling checker does that work now. What it misses is a correctly spelled word used for another, so `words.md` keeps the list's confusable pairs.

## This skill's reading

These are this skill's applications of Strunk, not sentences of his:

- The order of the steps (point, paragraphs, sentences, words, usage) and their completion criteria. The book puts usage first; the skill puts structure first, because it decides which sentences survive to be edited.
- Step 1's point sentence: Rule 10's topic sentence, applied to a text instead of a paragraph.
- Listing the topics before writing, and a paragraph to each however short, in step 2: Rule 9 says a subject of slight extent "may" need no subdividing, and this skill reads that as one topic, not few words. The example a reader can copy in docs is Rule 10's "giving illustrations or specific instances".
- Lists, tables, and headings in step 2. Strunk mentions only the table (Rule 15's "the form of a table"); the rest extends Rule 9's paragraph to markdown.
- The three branches. The reviewing branch takes its form from the Introductory: section numbers "used as references in correcting manuscript".
- Deferring to a voice or style guide that is loaded. The Introductory grants that "each instructor has his own body of theory, which he prefers to that offered by any textbook."
- Keeping code, commands, identifiers, error text, quotations, and names verbatim.
- Leaving out a default or a figure the sources don't give, and cutting a prediction nobody has measured, while keeping an illustration such as a sample config, which claims nothing: Rule 12's definite assertions, read with Chapter V's *fact*. Treating a condition or limit on a fact as part of it, so that neither Rule 12 nor Rule 13 cuts it.
- The caller's part: a calling skill can name the reader, the ask, and the point for step 1, and pass a keep-list to the revising branch that the rules never cut.
- Every "In agent prose" line in `composition.md`.
- In `words.md`: the test that a word able to sit in any sentence adds nothing to this one (from Chapter V's preface); *certainly* read as the rule for every intensifier; the sentence that any phrase meaning no more than a simple word gives way to it (from *student body*); and the confusable pairs from Chapter VI.
- In `composition.md`, Rule 12: hedges as the modern form of "hesitating, non-committal language"; the *not X but Y* turn losing its strength through overuse, after Rule 10's warning that a device "if too often used, would become a mannerism"; and giving the number when there is one, or cutting the prediction when there is none (Rule 12's definite assertions, read with Chapter V's *fact*, kept for "matters of a kind capable of direct verification").
- In `usage.md`: the blockquote for verse, links as references, and `*Title*` for italics.
