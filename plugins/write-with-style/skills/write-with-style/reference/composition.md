# Composition

Rules 9 to 18 of the 1918 text: what each asks, Strunk's own examples (before → after), and the exceptions he allows. Each heading is the rule as he worded it. The last line under each rule, "In agent prose", is this skill's reading of where the fault turns up today.

## 9. Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic

A paragraph to each topic is there "to aid the reader": "The beginning of each paragraph is a signal to him that a new step in the development of the subject has been reached." A subject "of slight extent", or one treated briefly, needs no dividing: "a brief description, a brief summary of a literary work, a brief account of a single incident, a narrative merely outlining an action, the setting forth of a single idea, any one of these is best written in a single paragraph." Once it is written, check whether dividing it would help. "The extent of subdivision will vary with the length of the composition": a short notice of a book takes one paragraph; one slightly longer takes two, an account of the work and a critical discussion; a historical event takes three, what led up to it, the event, and what it led up to.

- "As a rule, single sentences should not be written or printed as paragraphs." The exception is "sentences of transition, indicating the relation between the parts of an exposition or argument."
- In dialogue, each speech is a paragraph, "even if only a single word".
- In agent prose: Summary, Changes, and Impact headings over a subject of slight extent; several topics run together in one paragraph because each is short; a run of one-sentence paragraphs; an argument whose steps depend on each other, broken into bullets.

## 10. As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning

"Again, the object is to aid the reader." The reader learns "the purpose of each paragraph as he begins to read it" and keeps it in mind "as he ends it". The most generally useful paragraph, "particularly in exposition and argument", has:

- the topic sentence at or near the beginning;
- the sentences after it explaining, establishing, or developing it;
- a final sentence that "either emphasizes the thought of the topic sentence or states some important consequence."

"Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is particularly to be avoided." A word or phrase in the topic sentence (*again*, *therefore*, *for the same reason*) ties a paragraph to the one before; when the transition takes more than one sentence, it is "generally better" set apart as a paragraph of its own. The body develops the topic sentence by restating it, defining its terms, denying the converse, giving instances, proving it, or showing its consequences. In docs, the instance is often an example the reader can copy: a sample config or command.

- Narrative may open on "a concise, comprehensive statement" ("The breeze served us admirably."), but that device, "if too often used, would become a mannerism"; brief paragraphs of "animated narrative" may have no topic sentence at all.
- In agent prose: an opening that announces instead of stating ("This PR introduces…", "In this document we will…"); a closing recap or sign-off ("Overall, …", "In summary, …") where the paragraph should end on its consequence.

## 11. Use the active voice

"The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive."

- "My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me." → "I shall always remember my first visit to Boston." Dropping "by me" makes the passive "indefinite: is it the writer, or some person undisclosed, or the world at large, that will always remember this visit?"
- "There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground." → "Dead leaves covered the ground."
- "The sound of the falls could still be heard." → "The sound of the falls still reached our ears."
- "The reason that he left college was that his health became impaired." → "Failing health compelled him to leave college."
- "It was not long before he was very sorry that he had said what he had." → "He soon repented his words."

Avoid making one passive depend directly on another: "Gold was not allowed to be exported." → "It was forbidden to export gold (The export of gold was prohibited)." A noun that "expresses the entire action, leaving to the verb no function beyond that of completing the sentence" gives its action back to the verb:

- "A survey of this region was made in 1900." → "This region was surveyed in 1900."
- "Mobilization of the army was rapidly carried out." → "The army was rapidly mobilized."
- "Confirmation of these reports cannot be obtained." → "These reports cannot be confirmed."

The passive "is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary". The word that has to be the subject decides: "The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed to-day" belongs in a paragraph about those dramatists, "Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration" in a paragraph about modern readers.

- In agent prose: a passive that hides who acted ("the decision was made", "errors were encountered", "changes were made to the parser"); *there is* and *there are* openings; an action turned into a noun that the verb merely completes ("performs a migration of", "provides support for").

## 12. Put statements in positive form

"Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, non-committal language. Use the word not as a means of denial or in antithesis, never as a means of evasion."

- "He was not very often on time." → "He usually came late."
- "He did not think that studying Latin was much use." → "He thought the study of Latin useless."
- not honest → dishonest; not important → trifling; did not remember → forgot; did not pay any attention to → ignored; did not have much confidence in → distrusted.

"The reader is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; he wishes to be told what is." Antithesis is strong ("Not charity, but simple justice."), and so are negative words other than *not* ("The sun never sets upon the British flag.").

- In agent prose: hedges (*may potentially*, *could possibly*, *seems to*, *somewhat*, *arguably*) where the writer knows the answer; *not insignificant* and *not uncommon*; the *not X but Y* turn spent on so many sentences that it stops being strong. A definite assertion gives the number when there is one. When there is none, a predicted effect (*should be faster*, *users should see fewer errors*) goes, hedge and all: it is neither definite nor a fact.

## 13. Omit needless words

"Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell."

- The wordy forms, *the fact that* first, are in `words.md`.
- A single complex idea told step by step in a series of sentences goes into one sentence. Strunk's 55 words ("Macbeth was very ambitious. This led him to wish to become king of Scotland. The witches told him that this wish of his would come true. The king of Scotland at this time was Duncan. Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth murdered Duncan. He was thus enabled to succeed Duncan as king.") become 26 ("Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth achieved his ambition and realized the prediction of the witches by murdering Duncan and becoming king of Scotland in his place.").
- Positive statements and the active voice are shorter, so Rules 11 and 12 serve this rule too.
- In agent prose: a sentence that restates the one before it; a paragraph that restates its heading; a list that restates the paragraph above it.

## 14. Avoid a succession of loose sentences

The loose sentence meant here has "two co-ordinate clauses, the second introduced by a conjunction or relative": *and*, *but*, and less often *who*, *which*, *when*, *where*, and *while*. One is fine, and "an occasional loose sentence prevents the style from becoming too formal" (Rule 4); a series "soon becomes monotonous and tedious". Strunk's example, a notice of a concert, is bad "because of the structure of its sentences, with their mechanical symmetry and sing-song."

Recast enough of them to break the monotony: into simple sentences, sentences of two clauses joined by a semicolon, periodic sentences of two clauses, or sentences of three clauses, loose or periodic, "whichever best represent the real relations of the thought."

- In agent prose: paragraph after paragraph of *X, and Y* and *X, which Y* sentences of one length and one rhythm. The sing-song is the tell.

## 15. Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form

"Expressions of similar content and function should be outwardly similar. The likeness of form enables the reader to recognize more readily the likeness of content and function."

- "Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method, while now the laboratory method is employed." → "Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught by the laboratory method." The first "gives the impression that the writer is undecided or timid".
- An article or a preposition that applies to every member of a series goes before the first term only, or before each: "The French, the Italians, Spanish, and Portuguese" → "The French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese"; "In spring, summer, or in winter" → "In spring, summer, or winter (In spring, in summer, or in winter)".
- Correlatives (*both*, *and*; *not*, *but*; *not only*, *but also*; *either*, *or*; *first*, *second*, *third*) take the same construction after each: "It was both a long ceremony and very tedious." → "The ceremony was both long and tedious."; "A time not for words, but action" → "A time not for words, but for action"; "Either you must grant his request or incur his ill will." → "You must either grant his request or incur his ill will."; "My objections are, first, the injustice of the measure; second, that it is unconstitutional." → "My objections are, first, that the measure is unjust; second, that it is unconstitutional."

Varying the form for its own sake is the "mistaken belief" this rule corrects. A statement repeated for emphasis may vary its form, as Stevenson's does in the paragraph under Rule 10. Twenty similar ideas fall into groups, with the rule applied within each group, or else into "the form of a table".

- In agent prose: a bulleted list whose items mix a verb phrase, a sentence, a noun phrase, and a fragment; headings in mixed grammatical forms.

## 16. Keep related words together

"The position of the words in a sentence is the principal means of showing their relationship."

- Subject and verb stay together; an interruption that can open the sentence goes there instead: "Wordsworth, in the fifth book of The Excursion, gives a minute description of this church." → "In the fifth book of The Excursion, Wordsworth gives a minute description of this church." A relative clause, an expression in apposition, or a deliberate interruption that builds suspense in a periodic sentence (Rule 18) may stand between them.
- A relative pronoun comes right after its antecedent: "He wrote three articles about his adventures in Spain, which were published in Harper's Magazine." → "He published in Harper's Magazine three articles about his adventures in Spain."
- A modifier goes next to the word it modifies: "All the members were not present." → "Not all the members were present."; "He only found two mistakes." → "He found only two mistakes."
- Several modifiers of one word are arranged so that "no wrong relation is suggested": Strunk's lecture notice puts the day and hour first and gives the invitation a sentence of its own.
- In agent prose: a long clause between subject and verb; *only*, *just*, and *also* placed before the verb instead of before the word they limit; a *which* that points at the wrong noun.

## 17. In summaries, keep to one tense

A summary of a play takes the present; of a poem, story, or novel, preferably the present, though the past is allowed. Earlier action takes the perfect in a present-tense summary and the past perfect in a past-tense one. A past tense in indirect discourse or indirect question stays: "The Legate inquires who struck the blow." Otherwise, "Shifting from one tense to the other gives the appearance of uncertainty and irresolution."

- In summarizing someone's statements, say "at the outset, once for all, that what follows is summary", and drop the "he said", "he stated", "the speaker added", and "the author also thinks".
- In criticism, write "an orderly discussion supported by evidence, not a summary with occasional comment"; across several works, aim at general conclusions instead of taking the works one by one in order.
- In agent prose: a summary of a thread, a meeting, or a paper that hops between *says* and *said*, or tags every sentence with "the author notes"; a review that retells the PR instead of judging it.

## 18. Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end

"The proper place for the word, or group of words, which the writer desires to make most prominent is usually the end of the sentence."

- "Humanity has hardly advanced in fortitude since that time, though it has advanced in many other ways." → "Humanity, since that time, has advanced in many other ways, but it has hardly advanced in fortitude."
- "This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness." → "Because of its hardness, this steel is principally used in making razors."

The words that earn the end are "usually the logical predicate, that is, the new element in the sentence". A periodic sentence saves its main statement for the end. The beginning is the other prominent place: "Deceit or treachery he could never forgive." A subject gains emphasis by taking the predicate's place: "Through the middle of the valley flowed a winding stream." The principle "applies equally to the words of a sentence, to the sentences of a paragraph, and to the paragraphs of a composition."

- In agent prose: a sentence that ends on a qualifier, a citation, or a hedge ("…, in most cases", "…, as noted above") after its news; a paragraph that ends on a caveat when the caveat is not the point.
